Boccherini OMR Lab Report

Lab report · manuscript-labeler · 1–13 October 2026

Reading Boccherini's quartet manuscripts by machine

Bar-line detection and Zeus fine-tuning for the Op. 48 edition: every model trained, every data source, every result so far.

Summary. We want a machine reading of Boccherini's string-quartet manuscripts good enough to draft an edition's encoding. The pipeline has two learned stages: finding the bar lines (which cuts each staff into bars) and reading each staff's notes (Zeus, a handwritten-music reader from OmniOMR). Bar-line detection is close to solved on Boccherini's copyists: the labeler finds 99% of bar lines on pages no model has seen. Reading is not. Fine-tuning Zeus on encodings we already have raised a trained copyist's hand from 45% to 81% of bars exactly right, and an unseen copyist's from 55% to 65%. On non-Boccherini hands (OSSQ) the fine-tuned model is no better overall and narrower: better on one hand, worse on two. The gains come from labelled data in the hand being read; extra data from other hands adds little.

1. The task and the pipeline

The edition (Boccherini, Op. 48, G. 226–231) is encoded by hand in LilyPond from manuscript part-books. The labeler is a browser tool in which the editor confirms each page's staves, bar lines, clefs and marks. Those labels are the ground truth for the bar-line detectors. The edition's encodings, paired with the labelled bars, are the ground truth for the reader.

Pagescan or photo of a part-book page
Stavesrule-based tracing (detect.py); staff ends from YOLO26n
Bar linesvote, 2 of 3: the labeler's own (rules + learned filter), YOLO26n, YOLO11n
NotesZeus reads each staff to LMX (linearised MusicXML)
Pairingstaff ↔ encoded bars, by alignment (autopair, barpair)

The pairing step turns any encoding of a work into training data for its manuscripts, without hand labelling: each detected staff is lined up with the encoding's bars. A staff is kept only if the alignment holds up. On the three labelled sources it has kept 0 wrong staves.

7. What worked, what didn't, and why

Worked

  • Votes of unlike detectors. The learned filter and the YOLOs make different mistakes, so 2 of 3 removes most of each one's own.
  • Judging ink against the paper. It rescued the faint Paris B hand: a third of its bar lines had never become candidates.
  • Staff ends as detector classes. They cut the most-edited thing (a staff's right end) from 56% to 95% within a staff space.
  • A small learning rate (1e-5) for Zeus. The trained hand gains without losing the unseen one. At 1e-4 every run forgot the hands it hadn't seen.
  • Pairing encodings made elsewhere. Philhar's encodings became 2,000+ staves of training data with no labelling, and none wrong where we could check.
  • Labels from the encoding for bar lines (OSSQ). Filling in a missed line from the same line on the other staves taught a new hand: Maier 55% → 74% of staves right.

Didn't

  • Self-labelled bar lines (Philhar bootstrap). They showed only what the detector already found, and the volume swamped Op. 48.
  • Pretraining on other handwriting (MUSCIMA++). It helped familiar hands and hurt the unseen one.
  • Mixing in generic data for Zeus (Dolores). It made no difference once the learning rate was low (E ties F).
  • More of the same hands. F → G added 500 staves: the trained hand rose, the unseen hand stayed at 65%.
  • Reading with Claude in one pass, and oemer and segmentation for staves.
  • Adding Mayer A and more Philhar staves (run H). Vma moved 2 points, inside the noise; Maier and Andrée lost another 5–6 points.
  • Threshold tuning on the OSSQ pair: it traded a false bar line for more misses.

Why we think so

Both stages learn the hand, not music notation in general. Every gain on a hand came from labelled data in that hand: KHM's copyist from the Op. 48/1 encoding, the Philhar copyists from Philhar's encodings, Maier's bar lines from Andrée's and Mayer's. Data from other hands helps a new hand only when the hands resemble each other. Mayer A, whose writing is closer to the trained copyists, gained 11 points from G. Andrée and Maier lost some.

The unseen Vma copyist moved once, when the Philhar hands arrived (55% → 61–65%), and stayed flat through G and H (run H's 66–67% is within noise of G): more data from other hands didn't reach it. Its own bars did. Run I added 70 Vma staves from Op. 48/3 movement I and Vma II rose to 69–70%, five points over G, with Maier about level with G and Andrée three points below, a smaller narrowing than H's.

Zeus's errors are mostly durations in dense passagework and pitches one step off (a note head's place misjudged), not wrong clefs or keys. A LilyPond-style bar check catches 55–80% of rhythm errors. What it can't see, wrong pitches, needs a measurement of note heads rather than another reading.

8. Is this a paper yet?

Not yet. Candidate contributions:

  • Turning existing editions' encodings into training data for their manuscripts by alignment, with a measured wrong-pair rate of zero.
  • Bar-line labels for a new hand derived from an encoding (system-wide consistency plus bar counts), measured on a held-out hand.
  • A careful account of fine-tuning a handwritten-music reader per copyist: the learning-rate cliff, narrowing on other hands, and the limits of more data from the same hands.

What a reviewer would ask for:

  • One run per setting, with no seeds or error bars. Differences of 2–3 points are noise.
  • Small test sets: 8 bar-line pages, two Zeus movements.
  • Vma II has been watched since the sweep, so it is a dev set, not a test set. KHM 603 I (Op. 48/2) is now the clean test: G and I read 62–64% there against 81–84% on the dev movement KHM 602 II, so the dev numbers flatter.
  • The two OSSQ measures aren't comparable with compare.py.
  • Zeus's weights are non-commercial, so the fine-tuned models inherit CC BY-NC-SA.

A blog post fits now, as "what it takes to read one composer's manuscripts". A workshop paper (WoRMS, ISMIR LBD) fits once there's a clean held-out test and repeated runs.

9. Next directions

  1. Encode for each hand. Run I confirmed it: 70 staves of the Vma copyist's own bars moved Vma II five points, where 1,800 staves of other hands hadn't. Next, Op. 48/4 (Vma ms 1067 (2), RES 507 (16) autograph). Hold its first movement out too, as a second clean test in another hand.
  2. Clefs, before pitch by measurement. On the clean test, only 2–6 of 348 bars are wrong by one-step pitches alone, so measuring note heads (the edition's headcheck) is worth 2 points at most there. Whole passages shifted by a clef cost more: 11–19 bars (3–5 points). There are two kinds:
    • The cello's two treble-clef solos (bars 42–46, 79–82) had no clef mark in the labels, so the scoring read Zeus's notes there in bass clef. They are labelled now (2026-10-15), and the cello rose from 82% to 86–87%.
    • Viola passages (25–29, 62–67) and violin 2's bars 10–13 come out 4–6 steps off, in clefs the labels have right. Tried: limiting Zeus to the labelled clef. It doesn't help. Each staff was read one at a time, freely and limited to the labelled clef. Run I went from 64% to 65%, and run G from 62% to 61%.
      • Violin 2 gains 5–7 bars: two treble staves that Zeus read as alto now come out right.
      • The viola loses 4–7 bars. Zeus's wrong clef token on its own did no harm: it wrote tenor or treble on a viola staff but placed the notes correctly, and the scoring re-reads them in alto. Forcing alto sends the rest of the reading down a worse path.
      Limiting only the violins would be a rule fitted to the test movement; it would have to be chosen on the dev movements first.
  3. Noise: a bigger test, not more seeds. On the clean test, I beats G by 1.7 points, with a 95% interval of −1.8 to +5.5 (paired, resampled by 4-bar blocks). Only 34 bars differ between them. One movement can't tell runs apart within about 4 points, however many times each run is repeated. Repeat runs (about 2 hours each) are worth it only once the test is bigger: Op. 48/4 I, and later movements as they're encoded. Until then, treat differences under 4 points as ties.
  4. Bar lines through stems. The remaining misses on the autograph. Few in number; a targeted labelled set would do.
  5. A reader built on MuSViT. MuSViT is an image encoder pretrained on 9.7M IMSLP pages (ECCV 2026, CC BY-NC-SA, encoder weights only). Its staff-level setup reads 128 px staves, as Zeus does, and its released code trains a CTC sequence head with LoRA adapters, which could learn to write LMX for compare.py to score. Zeus's decoder can't be reused, so the head would train first on OLiMPiC, Dolores and OmniOMR, then on our staves. The question it answers: does an encoder that has seen far more hands cope with an unseen copyist better than Zeus? First test, about 2–3 days: the 25M light encoder on G's data plus Dolores, scored on Vma II and the clean test. Stop if it doesn't beat G there (65% and 62%). Its vectors could also, with no training, rank staves or IMSLP manuscripts by likeness to the Vma hand, if they turn out to capture the hand; that's untested.
